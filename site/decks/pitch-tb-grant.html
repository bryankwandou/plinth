A missed-dose text sends a health worker to the patient's door

TB treatment follow-up for Indonesia and the Philippines · grant proposal · [applicant name needed]

This proposal is not another reminder app. Patients get a daily text, and when they stop answering, a community health worker in their village gets an alert and visits within days. The text is the trigger. The visit is the intervention. We will run it in Indonesia and the Philippines, where the TB burden is heaviest in the region.
Need

Indonesia and the Philippines carry one in six TB cases worldwide

10% + 6.8%

Indonesia is second only to India. The Philippines is third. Together they account for more cases than China.

Source: WHO Global TB Report 2025, section 1.1 [H1]

These are WHO estimates for 2024. Worldwide, about 10.7 million people fell ill with TB that year. Indonesia's share is ten percent and the Philippines' is six point eight. If a program works in these two countries, it touches a large part of the global problem.
Need

Both countries also lead the world in patients the system misses

10% + 7.5%

Share of the global gap between estimated TB cases and cases officially notified in 2024.

Source: WHO Global TB Report 2025, section 2.3 [H4]

Indonesia ranks first on this list and the Philippines third. People who fall out of the system before or during treatment are the ones a village health worker can find. The globally reported treatment success rate is 88 percent, which means roughly one patient in eight does not finish well.
Evidence against us

Reminder texts alone did not change outcomes in Pakistan

2,207

Patients in a Karachi trial of daily SMS reminders. Clinic-reported outcomes showed no significant difference from the comparison group.

Source: J-PAL, Zindagi SMS evaluation [H5]

We looked for the evidence that hurts us first. In the Zindagi trial, reply rates fell from 48 percent in the first two weeks to about a fifth by the end. In a Chinese cluster trial, texts barely moved missed doses. A 2025 meta-analysis rates the pooled evidence for digital adherence tools as very low certainty.
Evidence against us

In China, texts barely moved missed doses; a pill monitor did

27.3% vs 17.0%

Patient-months with a fifth of doses missed, text arm vs medication-monitor arm. Control was 29.9%.

Source: Liu et al., PLOS Medicine 2015 [H7] [H8]

The lesson we take is that a text on its own is easy to ignore. What worked better was a device that measured doses and prompted a response from the clinic. Our design keeps the cheap text but makes silence visible to a person who can act on it.
Approach

We use the text to find who stopped, then send a person

  • Daily two-way SMS; the patient replies after each dose
  • Two silent days raise an alert to the assigned health worker
  • The worker visits, logs the reason, and the clinic adjusts care
Replies falling off is the data point Zindagi treated as failure. We treat it as the signal. The reason logged at each visit, whether side effects, cost of travel or stigma, becomes the dataset the ministries can use.

Impact

Success means beating the 88% global treatment success rate

88%

We report treatment success and loss to follow-up for both arms, against this baseline, whatever the result.

Source: WHO Global TB Report 2025, section 2.3 [H3]

Pooled trials of digital tools show at best a modest gain, an odds ratio of about 1.14 with an interval that touches no effect. We expect a small effect and have sized the evaluation to detect one, or to say clearly that it is not there.
Sustainability

If the visits work, the ministries keep the alerts

Open-source code, handed to national TB programs; funding available covered about a quarter of the 2027 target [H10]

Global TB funding is short: about 5.9 billion dollars in 2024 against a 22 billion target, as reported from the WHO report. That argues for a tool that is cheap to keep running and that ministries own, not one that needs us.
